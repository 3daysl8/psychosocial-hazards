Crossref identity checks establish which publication a DOI identifies; they do not establish that the publication supports every claim made about it.

## Ready-to-use replacement wording

### Summary point 3

> None of the sources examined validates the Guide's proposed hazard weights, percentage control discounts, interaction multipliers or risk-band thresholds. The Guide's ratings should therefore be presented as structured professional judgement, not calibrated probabilities of injury. Validated surveys can measure specific constructs, but their validation does not automatically transfer to a different tool or scoring rule.

### Evidence-table introduction

> The table presents selected pooled estimates and one single-cohort estimate from observational studies. OR compares odds; RR compares risk over a specified period; HR compares the event rate at a given time among people who have not yet experienced the event. Values above 1 indicate a positive association relative to each study's comparison group. These measures are not interchangeable and do not give an individual's absolute probability of harm. Brackets show 95% confidence intervals. The van der Molen values come from Table 3, which differs slightly from the paper's prose in some places.

### Victorian control rule

> In Victoria, information, instruction or training may be the sole control only where none of the specified changes to work, plant, systems, design or environment is reasonably practicable. Where controls are combined, information, instruction or training must not predominate.

### Score design

> The Guide's agreed design is to display an indicative rating for each hazard and use the highest rating as its overall summary. This is a design choice, not a regulator-prescribed or research-validated formula. Assessors must still examine combined exposures and decide what action is needed; the summary score cannot make that decision for them.

### Interventions paragraph

> Changes to work and support for individuals serve complementary purposes. Fleming's 2024 cross-sectional study found no better well-being associated with participation in most of the individual interventions examined; volunteering was an exception. The design cannot establish whether those interventions caused benefit or harm. WHO's 2022 Recommendation 1 conditionally supports consideration of organisational interventions addressing psychosocial risk factors, based on very low-certainty evidence. The guidelines also address manager training and individual interventions. The existence of a programme alone does not demonstrate that a workplace risk is adequately controlled.

Add citations to Fleming and WHO immediately after their respective statements.

### Original bibliography error statement

> The original report contained substantial citation errors. Of its 30 DOI-bearing references, four identifiers returned no Crossref record and six resolved to unrelated publications. Other entries required corrections to authors or publication details. A reference-by-reference register records the findings.

This replaces both occurrences of “13 of 32.” A registry 404 is a result for the supplied identifier, not proof that the intended paper does not exist.

### Evidence-gap paragraph

> Among the sources examined, we did not identify directly comparable pooled estimates for low job demands, remote or isolated work, poor physical environment, or exposure to traumatic events or material. For organisational change, the quantitative evidence used here comes from a single cohort. Other relevant literature uses different outcomes or correlation measures. Converting those results would require assumptions not established for the proposed comparisons. This narrative review does not establish that no other relevant evidence exists.

### Sojo exclusion statement

> Some source-specific estimates remain excluded. For Sojo, an accessible author manuscript reported values different from those in the original review; these have not been reconciled against the final published version. Other excluded estimates could not be verified from the available full text.

## Final editorial improvements

- Describe reports such as “I'm burnt out” as signals requiring investigation of possible hazards, rather than automatically identifying a specific hazard or proving its cause (line 57).
- Keep duration and severity examples separate: “a year” illustrates duration; the intensity or nature of the exposure illustrates severity (line 63).
- Describe the Model Code as this article's chosen starting point; “clearest” or “best” is an editorial judgement, not a comparative research finding. Prioritise the applicable jurisdiction's guidance.
- Add a short statement that source selection followed the earlier audit and was not a comprehensive search through October 2026. A few recent access dates do not establish an up-to-date evidence synthesis.
- Make the research and practical guidance the main public article. The AI process history can be a separate methods note. If retained, link readers to the actual correction log and distinguish documented events from lessons inferred by the author.
- Do not describe a source as verified solely because multiple AI systems agreed. Keep the source-location table with the working records and ensure the final text uses the corrected outcome and design labels.

**Publication decision:** retain the central narrative and verified citations; apply A1–A14, remove the WHO placeholder, and check the resulting final text. The draft is close enough for targeted revision, but the current version should not be labelled fully reference-checked or publication-ready without the qualifications and corrections above.
