# Assessing psychosocial hazards: what the research can and can't tell you

*An AI-assisted research review, audited by AI and checked against original sources. Prepared 24 October 2026 for the Psychosocial Hazard Investigation Guide. Revised the same day after a further audit (`research/assessing-psychosocial-hazards-review-audit.md`). Status: draft for owner review.*

This is a guide to methods, not legal advice. It doesn't tell you whether a duty has been met in any particular case. Check the law, Code or Compliance Code that applies where you work.

---

## In short

1. **How do you decide how serious a psychosocial hazard is?** Use your regulator's risk management process. For each hazard, work out who is exposed, how often, for how long and how severely, whether other hazards combine with it, and how well the current controls work. Do it with the workers affected and their HSRs. This review uses Safe Work Australia's Model Code as its starting point: its risk register template records exactly those things, hazard by hazard, with no formula.
2. **Research links these hazards with poorer mental health.** In the pooled studies examined, bullying, effort-reward imbalance, injustice, high and emotional demands, low support, violence and job insecurity were all associated with later depression or stress-related disorders.
3. **None of the sources examined validates a scoring formula.** None supports the proposed hazard weights, percentage control discounts, interaction multipliers or risk-band thresholds. A rating produced by a tool like this Guide is structured professional judgement, not a calibrated probability of injury. Validated surveys can measure specific constructs, but their validation doesn't carry over to a different tool or scoring rule.
4. **Missing research isn't low risk.** For remote or isolated work, low demands and poor physical environment, no directly comparable pooled estimate was identified in the sources examined. They are still hazards, and the Code still expects you to assess them.
5. **Assess combined exposures.** One Danish cohort found the odds of probable depression rose as the number of adverse work factors rose. That supports looking at hazards together. It doesn't give a multiplier.
6. **AI was useful for finding sources and unreliable for citing them.** Details are in the last section.

## The question: how does a person make the determination?

Anyone assessing psychosocial hazards, whether an HR practitioner, an HSR, a manager or a safety adviser, eventually has to answer: *how serious is this, and what needs to happen?*

### Why this review starts from the Safe Work Australia Model Code

Several frameworks exist: international standards, validated surveys, academic models, commercial tools. This review uses the Model Code of Practice: *Managing psychosocial hazards at work* (Safe Work Australia, 2022) as its starting point. That's an editorial choice, not a research finding, and the guidance for your own jurisdiction comes first. Four things make the Code a practical fit.

- **It's built for the decision you actually face.** It walks through identify, assess, control and review, the same risk management process used for physical hazards (Code, ch. 2, p. 12).
- **It asks for the information the research points to.** Duration, frequency, severity and combination are the dimensions the Code asks about, and the studies examined are consistent with risk rising along them. It doesn't ask you to rank bullying against workload with a number.
- **It's per hazard.** Its risk register template (Appendix C, p. 54) has one row per hazard and columns for how often, how long, how severe, what other hazards interact, how effective current controls are, and what more is needed. No formula, no total.
- **It keeps workers in the process.** Consultation with affected workers and HSRs runs through every step (Code, s 1.3 and ch. 2).

The Code only has legal effect in a jurisdiction where it has been approved, so check with your regulator.

**Victoria** has its own system: the OHS (Psychological Health) Regulations, in force from 1 December 2025, and the Compliance Code: Psychological health. Victoria lists 16 hazards rather than 14 (it separates high and low job demands and adds gendered violence) and uses employer and employee rather than PCBU and worker. It also limits how information, instruction or training can be used as a control. It may be the only control only where none of the specified changes to work, plant, systems of work, work design or the workplace environment is reasonably practicable. Where controls are combined, information, instruction or training must not be the predominant one (Regulations r 15(3)–(4); Compliance Code, Part 3, Step 3). The overall approach is consistent with the Model Code.

### The determination, step by step

This is our plain-English summary of the Code's process. Read the Code itself for the detail.

**1. Find the hazards.** Talk to workers and HSRs, observe how work is actually done, and review records: incidents, complaints, leave, turnover, hours worked, exit interviews, previous assessments (Code, s 3.2, pp. 19–21). People rarely name hazards in technical terms. "I'm burnt out", "I dread dealing with that client" and "the rules only apply to some people" are signals to look into: they point to possible hazards to investigate, without yet showing which hazard or what caused it. Look for less common but serious hazards too, such as assault, not just the everyday ones.

**2. For each hazard, describe the exposure.** The Code asks three questions (s 4.2, p. 23):

- *How long* does exposure last? (A single shift? A few weeks? A year?)
- *How often* does it happen? (Once? Weekly? Every shift?)
- *How severe* is it? (Rudeness, or a threat of violence? Pressure to work faster, or targets that can't be met at all?)

Risk is higher when exposure is more severe, more frequent or longer, and higher again when these combine. A single severe event can cause harm on its own.

**3. Look at hazards together.** Hazards interact. The Code's own example is a worker facing an aggressive customer, alone, with no say in how the work is done. That worker is at more risk than one facing the same customer with support and room to de-escalate (s 4.2). Note which hazards occur together and treat the combination as part of the assessment.

**4. Judge how well current controls work.** Ask whether each control is in place, used in practice and actually reducing exposure. The Code calls administrative controls the least reliable and says to use them with more effective controls (s 5.1, pp. 24–25). An EAP supports people who are already affected; on its own it doesn't remove the source of the hazard.

**5. Decide what more is needed.** The greater the risk, the more is expected. Eliminate the risk if that's reasonably practicable. Otherwise minimise it, looking first at changes to the work itself: design of work, systems of work, environment, interactions, tools. Cost counts only after the risk and the available controls have been weighed (s 5.1, pp. 24–26).

**6. Record it and review it.** Record hazards, assessment, controls, who acts and by when (ch. 7, p. 30). Review controls when they aren't working, before changes that could create new risks, when a new hazard is identified, when consultation shows a review is needed, or when an HSR asks because they reasonably believe one of those things has happened and it hasn't been adequately reviewed (ch. 6, p. 29). Complaints and grievances, including informal ones, are signals to review.

### Where a score fits

A score can help organise this thinking and make it more consistent between assessors. It can't make the decision. This Guide's agreed design is to show an indicative rating for each hazard and use the highest rating as its overall summary, so a serious single hazard can't be averaged away by several mild ones. That is a design choice, not a regulator-prescribed or research-validated formula. A highest-rating summary doesn't assess combined exposures by itself, so the Guide flags hazards that occur together, and the assessor still has to examine them and decide what action is needed. The rating is a summary of judgement, not a probability that someone will be injured, and not a finding about compliance.

### When to use a validated survey

For medium to large organisations, especially with higher psychosocial risk, the Code suggests considering a validated psychosocial risk assessment process (s 3.2, p. 20). Surveys help where anonymity matters, workers are spread across sites or shifts, or numbers are large. They add to consultation; they don't replace it. Things to check:

- **Benchmarks belong to their survey.** The Psychosocial Safety Climate scale (PSC-12) has organisational benchmarks: an average of 37 or below signals high risk, 41 or above low risk (Bailey, Dollard & Richards, 2015). Those are for organisations or work groups using that survey, not diagnostic cut-offs for an individual, and they can't be borrowed for a different tool or for this Guide's ratings.
- **Free to use isn't free to copy.** Check the permission terms before reproducing survey items in your own tool.
- **Availability changes.** The People at Work survey and online platform closed to organisations on 2 October 2026. Its other resources remain on the People at Work website.

## What the research shows

### Hazards linked with later mental ill-health

The table presents selected pooled estimates and one single-cohort estimate from observational studies. OR compares odds; RR compares risk over a specified period; HR compares the event rate at a given time among people who haven't yet had the event. Values above 1 indicate a positive association relative to each study's comparison group. These measures aren't interchangeable and don't give an individual's absolute probability of harm. Brackets show 95% confidence intervals. The van der Molen values come from its Table 3, which differs slightly from the paper's prose in some places (for example procedural justice 1.73, 1.61–1.86 in the text).

### How to read these numbers carefully

- **Different outcomes, different studies.** Depressive symptoms, diagnosed depression and stress-related disorders are different things. A higher number in one row doesn't make that hazard more harmful than another row measured differently.
- **Adjustment changes answers.** In Madsen's cohorts, job strain's HR of 1.27 fell to 1.03 (0.81–1.32) after adjusting for a continuous baseline depressive-symptom score. Whether that adjustment is right is debated, which is the point: the model matters.
- **Not every measure shows a clear association.** Low decision latitude (1.07, 0.92–1.25) and low skill discretion (1.11, 0.94–1.32) showed no clear association in van der Molen. Long hours showed no clear association in the Australian studies Virtanen pooled (0.95, 0.70–1.29).
- **Causation is uncertain.** The authors of a 2021 systematic review (Mikkelsen) conclude they can't say with confidence that any of these exposures causes depressive episodes, because of possible bias and confounding. That is a statement about the strength of evidence, not a finding that these hazards can't cause harm, and it doesn't weaken the case for prevention. It does mean the numbers can't be read as precise doses of harm.
- **Reviews overlap.** Several reviews reuse the same cohorts. A second review agreeing isn't always independent confirmation.

### Combined exposures

In a Danish prospective study (Lunen et al. 2023; 2,158 participants), the odds of probable depression over six months rose with the number of four work factors a person was exposed to: quantitative demands, emotional demands, role conflict and workplace bullying. The odds ratios were 2.98 for one factor, 3.14 for two, 6.44 for three and 9.62 for all four, with wide intervals (four factors: 3.30–28.01). This is consistent with the Code's advice to look at hazards together. It's one cohort, and it doesn't test whether hazards multiply each other's effects, so it can't supply a multiplier.

### Evidence gaps

Among the sources examined, we didn't identify directly comparable pooled estimates for **low job demands (underload)**, **remote or isolated work**, **poor physical environment**, or **exposure to traumatic events or material**. For organisational change, the quantitative evidence used here comes from a single cohort. Other relevant literature, for example on role conflict, role ambiguity, harassment and workplace conflict, mostly uses different outcomes or correlation measures. Converting those results would need assumptions whose suitability for this comparison hasn't been established. This narrative review doesn't establish that no other relevant evidence exists.

None of that means these hazards are minor. The Code lists all of them, and they should be assessed the same way as the rest.

### What the research doesn't support

The Gemini report proposed, and called evidence-based, several scoring features that none of the checked sources supports:

- a ranking of hazards from "most" to "least" harmful across all outcomes;
- converting correlations into odds ratios to fill gaps;
- a 1.25 "interaction multiplier" or a +15% "amplifier";
- fixed control discounts of 10%, 25% or 60%;
- risk bands described as probabilities of injury.

What the sources and the Code do support is narrower: duration, frequency and severity of exposure all matter, and a single severe exposure can cause harm. Controlling hazards and supporting affected people serve complementary purposes. The specific numbers in the report had no source.

### Interventions

Changes to work and support for individuals serve complementary purposes. Fleming's 2024 cross-sectional study of UK workers found that taking part in most of the individual-level interventions examined wasn't associated with better well-being; volunteering was an exception (Fleming 2024). The design can't establish whether those interventions caused benefit or harm. The World Health Organization's 2022 guidelines conditionally recommend considering organisational interventions that address psychosocial risks, based on very low-certainty evidence (WHO 2022, Recommendation 1, p. 12). The guidelines rate other approaches separately: training managers to support workers' mental health is a strong recommendation based on moderate-certainty evidence (Recommendations 4 and 5), and most individual interventions are conditional recommendations based on low- or very low-certainty evidence. The existence of a program, such as an EAP or a training course, doesn't by itself show that a workplace risk is adequately controlled.

## Recommendations

### For anyone assessing psychosocial hazards

1. Follow your regulator's process (the Model Code where it applies, or Victoria's Regulations and Compliance Code) and use its risk register or one like it.
2. Assess each hazard on duration, frequency, severity, combination with other hazards, and how well controls work in practice.
3. Act on a serious hazard on its own merits, whatever any overall score says.
4. Treat worker reports as evidence to investigate. Consult throughout, including HSRs.
5. Look first at changes to the work. Training, policies and support services usually work alongside those changes; check the rules in your jurisdiction (Victoria limits when they can be the only or main control).
6. Don't drop a hazard because research on it is thin.
7. Record decisions and review them when things change or controls aren't working.

### For anyone building an assessment tool

1. Say what decision the tool supports before choosing any formula.
2. Rate hazards separately and let the worst one show. Don't average. Still prompt users to examine hazards that occur together.
3. Don't present weights, discounts or bands as evidence-based. Testing against real workplace assessments can show agreement and usability; it doesn't by itself validate them as injury probabilities or show that controls work.
4. Label every output as indicative, and say plainly that it isn't a probability, a diagnosis or a compliance finding.
5. Check permissions before reproducing any validated survey.

### For this Guide

- Phase 5 is to use **per-hazard indicative ratings**, with the highest rating as the overall summary, combinations flagged, and no weights, multipliers or control discounts. (Owner decision, 24 October 2026; not yet built.)
- The Resources page can draw on the verified estimates above to explain *why* each hazard matters, with outcome and design stated next to every number.

## What we learned about doing research with AI

These are observations from this project, not general rules about how AI behaves. Four AI systems took part before this revision, and ChatGPT 6 Astra then audited this review as well. Each one was useful, and each one got things wrong.

**1. Instructions didn't stop fabrication.** The brief told Gemini never to estimate or infer a figure and to write "Not found" when it couldn't find something. It did write "Not found" for several hazards, correctly. It also produced a quotation attributed to the Safe Work Australia Code that isn't in the Code, a High Court case summarised wrongly, and substantial citation errors (see "How this review was made"). In this project, careful instructions improved the output but didn't make it safe to use unchecked.

**2. The numbers were more reliable than the labels around them.** Most effect sizes copied from open-access papers were correct. The errors clustered in the metadata: authors, journals, DOIs, page numbers. At least one correct figure was attached to the wrong paper: the bullying OR of 2.82 belongs to Theorell, not to the study it was credited to. Check *which paper* and *what number* as two separate questions.

**3. Every audit added errors of its own.** Our first audit wrongly called a real figure wrong (Madsen's 1.22), misspelt an author as "van Lunen", treated a defective citation as a nonexistent paper when a likely intended real paper exists (Fox et al. 2022, *Work & Stress*), and over-read one study as proof that hazards combine "less than additively". The second audit got a sample size wrong by about 160,000, gave the Safe Work Australia Code the wrong licence, and offered a new set of weights that were no better validated than the ones it criticised. The arbitration caught these. Seven of its rulings were then checked against the original sources, and all seven held up.⁴

**4. Confident wording didn't guarantee accuracy.** The second audit labelled findings "catastrophic" and "verified" in red and bold, and still contained errors. Only the source location (a table, a page, a line of text) settled anything.

**5. The brief shaped the answer.** We asked for evidence to support hazard weights, and every system worked inside that frame. Gemini proposed weights. Our audit recommended a weight set. The second audit produced replacement weights. The arbitration was the first step to say the frame itself wasn't supported, and the practical answer was already in the regulator's Code: assess each hazard on its own exposure and controls. In this project, asking an AI to strengthen a method produced a stronger-looking version of the same method. Whether the method was right needed asking as a separate question.

**6. Different models disagreeing was useful, but not as a vote.** Where the audits disagreed, that pointed to where to look. The disagreement was resolved by reading the paper, not by counting which side had more models. Agreement between AI systems wasn't treated as verification either.

**7. What actually worked:**

- resolving every DOI against the registry (Crossref) to confirm the paper's identity;
- pulling open-access full text (Europe PMC) and searching for each figure;
- recomputing every conversion and calculation in a script;
- searching the regulator's own document for any quoted passage;
- dropping figures that couldn't be checked, instead of carrying them forward.

**8. Access limits what can be checked.** Some estimates remain excluded. For Sojo 2016, an author manuscript reported values different from those in the original review, and they haven't been reconciled with the final published version. Others (Willness 2007, Brewin 2000) couldn't be verified from available full text. The WHO guideline web page blocked automated access, so the project owner supplied the PDF and the ratings were read from it directly. A narrower review with fewer, checked numbers is worth more than a long one with unchecked ones.

**The short version:** AI made a good map of where to look. The territory still had to be walked by reading the sources.

⁴ Checked by Claude Code against the original sources on 24 October 2026: (1) Madsen 1.22 (1.02–1.47) is in the paper's results; (2) Lunen is the first author's surname; (3) Lunen's one-factor interval is printed as 1.09–4.44; (4) Fox et al. 2022 exists in *Work & Stress* 36(1); (5) Gerhardt et al. 2021 pooled 387,407 participants; (6) PubMed record 42732758 is a real 2026 meta-analysis; (7) Rugulies's least-adjusted 1.68 (1.40–2.01) is in the paper.
