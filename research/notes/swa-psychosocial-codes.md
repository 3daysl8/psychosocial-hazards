### Section 1.1: what a psychosocial hazard is (summary)

Hazards arising from the design or management of work, the working environment, plant at a workplace, or workplace interactions or behaviours, that may cause psychological and physical harm. Harm includes conditions such as anxiety, depression, PTSD and sleep disorders, and physical injury (e.g. musculoskeletal injury, chronic disease, fatigue-related incidents). Stress itself is not an injury, but frequent, prolonged or severe stress can cause harm. Fear that a hazard (e.g. violence) hasn't been controlled can itself cause prolonged stress.

### Section 3.1: the 14 common hazards (names exactly as in the Code)

1. Job demands (includes sustained *low* demands and long idle periods, not just high demands)
2. Low job control
3. Poor support
4. Lack of role clarity
5. Poor organisational change management
6. Inadequate reward and recognition
7. Poor organisational justice
8. Traumatic events or material
9. Remote or isolated work
10. Poor physical environment
11. Violence and aggression
12. Bullying
13. Harassment including sexual harassment
14. Conflict or poor workplace relationships and interactions

The Code says the list and examples aren't exhaustive, that workers are usually exposed to a combination of hazards, that one severe instance can cause harm, and that how hazards are grouped matters less than a shared understanding of what's happening.

**Quoted definitions** (attribute to the Code, section 3.1):

- Bullying: "Repeated unreasonable behaviour directed towards a worker or group of workers that creates a risk to health and safety."
- Sexual harassment: "any unwelcome sexual advance, unwelcome request for sexual favours or other unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature, in circumstances where a reasonable person, having regard to all the circumstances, would anticipate the possibility that the person harassed would be offended, humiliated or intimidated."

Harassment also covers harassment based on personal characteristics (age, disability, race, nationality, religion, political affiliation, sex, relationship status, family or carer responsibilities, sexual orientation, gender identity, intersex status), and harmful behaviour that doesn't amount to bullying (such as single instances) but creates a risk.

### Appendix B: harmful behaviours (summary)

Sources can be external (customers, clients, patients, public, other businesses) or internal (workers, supervisors, managers). Low workforce diversity, some workforce characteristics (new, young, casual, minority-group workers) and a culture that tolerates harmful behaviour make it more likely. Subtle forms are harder to spot and taken less seriously.

## 2. Model Code of Practice: Health care and social assistance industry

- Page (psychosocial hazards chapter): https://www.safeworkaustralia.gov.au/book/health-care-and-social-assistance/hazards-risks-and-controls/psychosocial-hazards
- Industry-specific. Groups hazards into 6 broad types (high job demands, low job control, poor support, harmful behaviours from other workers, traumatic events or material, remote or isolated work). Says the language matters less than identifying what could cause harm.
- Licence of the web page: TO-CHECK (SWA copyright page).

### Useful for later phases (summarised)

- **Matters to consider when choosing controls** (the page calls it a specific legal duty): duration, frequency and severity of exposure; how hazards interact or combine; design of work; systems of work; design, layout and conditions of the workplace and workers' accommodation; equipment, substances and structures; workplace interactions or behaviours; information, training, instruction and supervision. Use in Phases 3, 5 and 7. The regulation number is TODO-VERIFY (model WHS Regulations).
- **EAP and counselling respond to harm that has already happened; they don't meet the duty to eliminate or minimise risk.** Supports putting individual supports at the bottom of the controls hierarchy (Phase 7).
- **Short-term vs long-term controls:** short-term measures (adjusting rosters, redeploying, deferring tasks) may be acceptable briefly, but over time more must be done if reasonably possible (e.g. more staff). Prolonged exposure raises what's expected. Phase 7.
- **Words workers use:** stressed, burnt-out, emotionally exhausted, anxious or scared, humiliated or undermined, angry about unfairness, confused about their role, "feeling like a failure", distressed or unable to sleep. Phase 2 triage and Phase 3, as signals of hazards rather than as "subjective" accounts.
- **Harmful behaviour between colleagues can be a response to other hazards** (high demands, unclear roles, poor support). Fix those first. Phase 4 systemic investigation.
- **Case studies:** in-home disability support (unassessed home-visit risks, no consultation, rising turnover; fixed by consultation, HSR elections, pre-visit risk checks) and a day surgery clinic (staff incivility traced to scheduling systems, admin vacancies and ad hoc training). Good models for Phase 8 scenarios, rewritten in our own words.
